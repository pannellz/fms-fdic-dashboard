## Client: Armstrong Bank
## Report Date: February 2021
## Source: FMS Brand Discovery Report

---

### Background & History

Armstrong Bank traces its origins to 1910, when it was founded as Vian State Bank in Vian, Oklahoma (Sequoyah County). The bank has been family-owned by the Armstrong family throughout its history. The name was changed from Vian State Bank to Armstrong Bank as the institution expanded beyond Vian into neighboring communities where the "Vian" name carried local rivalries (e.g., football competition between towns).

The bank's growth trajectory accelerated significantly through acquisitions. Key milestones include:

- Acquisition of the Warner, Oklahoma bank (after that institution went bankrupt)
- Expansion into Norman, Oklahoma through a separate investment that later became part of the Armstrong portfolio
- Acquisition of Benefit Bank (timing not specified, but pre-2020)
- Merger with Republic Bank (completed March 2020) -- Republic Bank was a "sister bank" under the same ownership in the Norman/OKC market, with a strong independent brand identity
- By the time of this report, Armstrong Bank had crossed $2 billion in assets
- In 2020 alone, the bank added 10 new branches through acquisition activity

The founding family remains actively involved. Sinclair Armstrong (referenced as the principal owner) maintains a hands-on role. The ownership philosophy emphasizes conservative management, humility, community service, and treating all customers equally regardless of economic status. Leadership has noted the bank has consistently ranked in the top 1% of performing banks for 30 years.

### Geographic Footprint & Market Characteristics

Armstrong Bank operates across two states with a split between legacy rural markets and newer urban/suburban growth markets:

**Legacy Markets (Eastern Oklahoma -- rural):**
- Vian (home market, Sequoyah County -- 41% market share, #1 position)
- Sallisaw, Gore, Muldrow, Warner, Wagoner, Muskogee, Collinsville, Bartlesville, Pawnee, Tahlequah area
- These communities are characterized as small-town, tight-knit, lower-income, agricultural/blue-collar
- The bank is the dominant or sole institution in several of these towns

**Growth Markets (Metro Oklahoma and Western Arkansas):**
- Oklahoma City / Edmond / Moore (entered via Republic Bank merger)
- Norman, Oklahoma (entered via early separate investment, later folded into Armstrong)
- Fort Smith, Arkansas (entered circa 2015 via acquisition)
- Springdale / Fayetteville / Northwest Arkansas (Bentonville-adjacent market with young Walmart/vendor executive demographic)
- These markets are more competitive, more affluent, more metropolitan, and have significantly lower brand awareness for Armstrong

The bank's footprint therefore spans very diverse economic environments -- from markets where it is effectively the only bank to highly saturated metro areas where it is largely unknown.

### Organizational Structure & Leadership

Armstrong Bank is privately held and family-owned. The governance structure includes a Board of Directors and an executive management team. Strategic planning occurs annually at an executive retreat. Key organizational characteristics:

- Local decision-making authority is pushed down to market and branch level, a deliberate strategic choice
- The bank operates without a centralized call center model (though the Republic merger introduced one, creating friction)
- Succession planning is an active priority for the leadership team
- The bank employs a regional structure with presidents/leaders in each market
- Lines of business include retail banking, commercial/business banking, mortgage lending, and treasury management
- Marketing is centralized and has been perceived internally as inflexible to local market needs

### Strategic Context

**Why the Rebrand:**
The brand discovery was commissioned because Armstrong Bank's rapid growth through acquisitions created several compounding problems:

1. **Brand fragmentation** -- The Republic Bank merger was particularly disruptive. Republic had a strong, independent community brand in Norman/South OKC. Employees and customers experienced the transition poorly: customer service changes, new fees, loss of local phone answering, and cultural clashes. Benefit Bank's integration also left residual fragmentation. Employees described feeling "branch-centered rather than bank-centered."

2. **Cultural dilution** -- Republic Bank had a different customer service philosophy (perceived as catering to upper-class customers based on economic status), which conflicted with Armstrong's egalitarian culture. The Armstrong values had not been effectively communicated to acquired employees.

3. **Low brand awareness in growth markets** -- In OKC, Norman, Fort Smith, and NW Arkansas, the Armstrong name carries almost no recognition. Republic Bank's strong local brand equity was lost in the name change without Armstrong building replacement awareness.

4. **Outdated brand presentation** -- The existing logo and visual identity were acknowledged as a quick two-week effort from 2015 with no strategic foundation. Employees and customers perceived some brand elements as dated and rural, not appealing to younger metro demographics.

5. **Inconsistent customer experience** -- Policies, procedures, and service levels varied significantly branch to branch, creating confusion when customers visited multiple locations.

**Growth Strategy:**
- The bank pursues growth through both acquisitions and organic expansion, with a stated preference for conservative, measured growth
- Leadership explicitly stated they have maintained low debt and strong capital positions specifically to be positioned for acquisition opportunities
- The bank is actively evaluating acquisition targets through consultants, filtering for cultural and geographic fit
- Organic growth targets are modest: approximately 3% annual loan growth was the stated 2021 goal
- Leadership views continued growth as necessary to sustain the product and technology investments required to remain competitive
- The owner stated directly that the bank is "not finished yet" and intends to continue growing

**Target Customer Segments:**
- The bank does not define a single typical customer -- executives acknowledged the customer base is highly diverse across markets
- Legacy markets serve teachers, ranchers, farmers, small business owners, blue-collar workers, and multi-generational family relationships
- Growth markets increasingly serve professionals, small-to-mid-size businesses, and younger demographics (late 20s to early 40s in NW Arkansas)
- Commercial banking and small business lending are identified as priority growth areas for both sides of the balance sheet
- The bank does not pursue C&I/receivable lending

### Competitive Positioning & Differentiation

**Core differentiators identified in research:**
- Family ownership creating authentic community commitment (not corporate obligation)
- Local decision-making authority at the branch/market level enabling faster response times
- Flexibility in lending -- willingness to look at deals other banks decline, ability to customize solutions outside rigid credit boxes
- Relationship depth -- bankers know customers by name, provide personal cell numbers, respond immediately
- Community involvement that is genuinely embedded (employees volunteer, attend local events, serve on boards)
- Financial strength and longevity (100+ years, top 1% performance for 30 years)

**Key Competitors by Market:**
- Fort Smith: First National Bank (aggressive lending, higher risk appetite), Arvest (strong deposit gatherer), Simmons Bank
- Oklahoma City: MidFirst, BancFirst (strong community brand with excellent local TV marketing), First United Bank (strong social media)
- Norman: Legacy Republic Bank brand loyalty still lingering with competitors
- NW Arkansas: Arvest, Security Bank, Simmons, Legacy National Bank, United Bank, Farmers and Merchants -- approximately 7-8 competitors
- Legacy markets: Limited competition; Armstrong is dominant

**Competitive vulnerabilities:**
- Arvest consistently cited as superior at attracting deposits, possibly due to branch network density
- BancFirst recognized as having best-in-class community-focused marketing in OKC
- First United Bank cited for strong social media execution
- National banks and credit unions compete on technology, longer amortization terms, and investment property lending

### Market Dynamics

**Economic Conditions:**
- Research was conducted during the COVID-19 pandemic, which reduced in-person banking, caused lobby closures, and disrupted normal business development activities
- NW Arkansas is a high-growth market driven by Walmart headquarters and its vendor community, attracting young professionals
- Legacy rural Oklahoma markets are stable but offer limited organic growth potential
- The pandemic accelerated customer expectations for digital banking capabilities

**Competitive Threats:**
- Continued bank consolidation nationally creates both acquisition opportunities and the risk of larger competitors entering Armstrong's markets
- Digital-native banking experiences (customers now compare community bank apps to Amazon/Apple)
- Credit unions competing on terms (longer amortizations, investment property lending) that Armstrong's conservative credit policies don't match
- Employee turnover threatening relationship-based competitive advantage -- customers specifically cited turnover as a reason they might leave
- The average customer uses 2.9 banks, meaning Armstrong is always being compared

**Opportunities Identified:**
- Cross-selling to existing customers (many customers unaware of full product suite)
- Deepening business banking relationships to impact both sides of the balance sheet
- Increasing electronic services adoption to reduce operational costs and improve retention
- Bank-at-Work programs (not yet formalized despite successful one-off efforts)
- Reallocating marketing spend from legacy markets (where dominance is established) toward growth markets (where awareness is needed)

### Brand Perception (Pre-Rebrand)

**How customers perceived Armstrong Bank:**
- Overwhelmingly positive in legacy markets: customers described the bank as family-like, caring, responsive, and deeply embedded in their communities
- Customers valued personal relationships above all other factors -- knowing their banker by name, having direct cell phone access, receiving fast responses
- Flexibility in lending was frequently cited as a reason customers chose and stayed with Armstrong
- Community involvement was noticed and appreciated by both customers and noncustomers

**Weaknesses identified in research:**
- Online banking platform described as "awkward" with frequent lockout issues
- Mobile app viewed as dated; the bank had actually regressed some customers to an older app version
- Lack of e-signature capability was a significant frustration for both employees and customers
- Employee turnover eroding the relationship advantage -- customers in multiple markets reported no longer seeing familiar faces
- In Norman/OKC specifically, the Republic-to-Armstrong transition damaged trust: new fees appeared, local phone answering was replaced with a call center, and the strong Republic community identity was lost without adequate replacement
- Compensation not perceived as competitive, contributing to staffing challenges and turnover
- Noncustomers in growth markets had minimal knowledge of Armstrong Bank; some confused its TV advertising with other brands (one spouse thought it was a Braum's commercial)

**Sentiment from noncustomers:**
- Those familiar with the bank through community involvement had positive impressions of its people and values
- Former Republic Bank customers/contacts felt the transition was poorly handled and brand equity was squandered
- In smaller communities, even noncustomers recognized Armstrong's community involvement as exceptional
- In metro markets, awareness was near zero -- the bank was perceived as a small rural institution

### Key Takeaways for Financial Analysis

**Factors that would explain deposit growth or decline:**
- The Republic Bank merger (March 2020) brought in a significant deposit base from Norman/OKC, but customer attrition risk is elevated due to transition friction, fee changes, and loss of local service identity
- Arvest is specifically identified as the primary deposit competitor and superior deposit gatherer
- The bank does not require deposits with loans but has an "expectation" of deposit relationships
- Customer dissatisfaction with digital banking (online platform, mobile app) could drive deposit attrition to competitors with better technology
- Employee turnover breaks the personal relationships that are the primary retention mechanism

**Branch expansion/contraction plans:**
- The bank added 10 branches in 2020 alone through the Republic merger
- Leadership is actively positioned for further acquisitions -- strong capital, low debt, engaged with deal-flow consultants
- Growth targets include the OKC metro, NW Arkansas corridor, and potentially additional eastern Oklahoma/western Arkansas markets
- No branch closures or contraction discussed; the strategy is explicitly growth-oriented
- Geographic filtering criteria for acquisitions include cultural fit and contiguous/proximate markets

**Stated financial goals or performance indicators:**
- Approximately 3% annual loan growth target (stated for 2021)
- Deposit growth goals described as "similar" to loan growth targets
- Crossed $2 billion in total assets by early 2021
- Consistently ranked in top 1% of performing banks for 30 years (per executive statement)
- Efficiency ratio is an acknowledged challenge given the community banking model and number of employees per million dollars of assets
- Leadership explicitly prioritizes profitability as the engine for capital growth, which funds further acquisition capacity
- No specific ROA, ROE, or capital ratio targets were disclosed in this report
- The bank's conservative credit standards (avoiding C&I/receivable lending, maintaining strict risk profiles) may result in lower loan yields but also lower credit losses relative to more aggressive competitors
