# ClientContext: Killbuck Savings Bank
### Brand Discovery Report Summary — Financial Marketing Solutions, August 2025

---

## Background & History

Killbuck Savings Bank was founded in **1900** and is celebrating its **125th anniversary** in 2025. The bank has spent more than a century serving **Holmes County, Ohio**, and surrounding communities. It is a **locally owned, independent community bank** with deep roots in a region characterized by a significant Amish population alongside non-Amish residents.

The bank's identity has been built on conservative banking practices, multigenerational customer relationships, and a family-first internal culture. Generations of families have banked here, and the institution is described as a "household name" in its core market. Local ownership is a consistent point of pride for employees, directors, and customers alike. Decisions are made locally — not by a distant corporate office — which is a defining operational and cultural characteristic.

---

## Geographic Footprint & Market Characteristics

- **Primary market:** Holmes County, Ohio — the bank's founding and headquarters market
- **Secondary markets:** Knox County and surrounding northeastern Ohio counties
- **Active branch locations with confirmed focus group research:** Berlin, Killbuck, and Wooster (Wayne County)
- **Market character:** The region has a substantial **Amish population** whose banking needs differ fundamentally from those of modern digital banking customers. Amish customers do not use digital tools; service delivery for this segment requires in-person visits to customer homes and phone-based communication. The bank has developed institutional competency in serving this dual customer base over 125 years.
- **Wooster presence** represents a more urbanized, competitive market where awareness of the bank is lower and where noncustomer perceptions skew toward viewing Killbuck as too small or rural.
- The bank is expanding beyond its traditional Holmes County core, which is creating brand awareness gaps and internal culture fragmentation across newer locations.

## Organizational Structure & Leadership

The report references the following internal stakeholder groups, all of whom participated in discovery research:

- **Executives** (participated in focus groups and written surveys)
- **Directors / Board of Directors** (written survey participants; described as community-embedded, locally committed)
- **Department heads and team leads:** Business Banking, Retail Banking, Mortgage, Marketing, HR, IT

No individual executives are named by title in the sections provided, though one executive is directly quoted: *"We care deeply about our customers. We want them to be ultra successful."*

**Key organizational observations:**
- Leadership maintains an open-door policy and is described as approachable at all levels, from CFO to call center staff
- The board is described as actively involved in community support and aligned with a locally owned mission
- HR and training are identified as organizational pain points, particularly around change management and onboarding consistency
- IT infrastructure is flagged as a challenge area (outdated systems, frustrating phone system)

## Strategic Context

### Why the Rebrand
The bank is undertaking a brand discovery and subsequent repositioning for several interconnected reasons:

1. **Growth beyond the core footprint** — As the bank expands into markets like Wooster, it lacks the name recognition and brand clarity needed to compete
2. **No formal mission or vision statement** — There is currently no documented brand platform to rally employees around, leading to inconsistency across branches and departments
3. **Generational customer and employee transitions** — The bank must appeal to younger generations while retaining its traditional base, including Amish customers
4. **Internal fragmentation** — Culture, training, communication, and service delivery have become inconsistent as the bank has grown
5. **External misperception** — Noncustomers and prospects in newer markets perceive the bank as small, outdated, and too rural for sophisticated banking needs

### Growth Strategy
- Expand presence in **northeastern Ohio** markets beyond Holmes County
- Retain local ownership structure while growing to a scale that makes independence sustainable
- Modernize technology and digital capabilities without alienating traditional (including Amish) customers
- Develop stronger internal training, communication, and leadership development infrastructure to support growth
- Build brand visibility through more intentional community presence and marketing

### Target Segments
- **Existing Amish and rural customer base** — retain through continued culturally-adapted service
- **Non-Amish retail customers** in Holmes, Knox, and Wayne counties
- **Small businesses** — particularly those underserved by larger banks, including unconventional loan structures and local decision-making
- **New market residents and younger generations** in expanding footprint areas like Wooster

## Competitive Positioning & Differentiation

### Core Differentiators (confirmed by multiple stakeholder groups)
1. **Local ownership and local decision-making** — faster, more flexible responses than regional or national competitors
2. **Personalized, relationship-driven service** — customers are known by name; described as "intensely personal" and "kitchen table conversations"
3. **Cultural agility with Amish community** — 125 years of institutional knowledge in serving a unique dual customer base; some employees have Amish heritage
4. **Community investment** — monetary donations, employee volunteerism, event sponsorships (e.g., baseball teams, Sugar Creek events)
5. **Non-conventional lending flexibility** — creative mortgage and small business loan structures for customers who don't fit traditional molds
6. **Family-first internal culture** — low internal conflict, collaborative atmosphere, management accessibility

### Named Competitors
- Competitors are referenced but not named individually in the sections provided; however, the report notes: *"We don't set up the booth at the county fair anymore. Our competition is there, but we aren't"* — indicating active local competitors with visible community presence
- Larger out-of-town banks are referenced as a competitive foil — the local/personal model is positioned explicitly against them

### Competitive Vulnerabilities
- Weak brand awareness outside the Holmes County core
- Technology gaps (phone systems, digital tools) relative to competitors
- Inconsistent service delivery across branches undermines the personal service claim
- Limited marketing visibility; the bank does not effectively communicate its community contributions — *"We don't toot our own horn"*

## Market Dynamics

### Economic and Community Conditions
- Holmes County's economy is heavily influenced by the Amish community, which drives demand for non-digital, in-person, and relationship-based banking
- The region is described as rural-to-small-town in character, with Wooster representing the most urbanized market in the footprint
- Small business and agricultural lending appear to be significant components of the loan portfolio, consistent with the regional economy

### Threats
- **Larger regional and national banks** with greater technology investment and brand recognition in growth markets
- **Employee turnover** in customer-facing roles degrading the service quality that is the bank's primary differentiator
- **Technology obsolescence** — outdated systems and a frustrating phone experience are cited as customer and employee friction points
- **Demographic shift** — younger generations expect digital-first banking; failure to modernize risks losing the next generation of customers
- **Growth-induced culture dilution** — as the bank expands, the family culture and service consistency that define the brand become harder to maintain

### Opportunities
- **Underserved small business market** — businesses that don't fit conventional bank molds
- **Noncustomer conversion in existing markets** — even in Killbuck and Wooster, noncustomers expressed openness to the bank, with community involvement cited positively
- **Brand visibility uplift** — significant unrealized equity; better storytelling and marketing could substantially improve awareness and consideration
- **Workforce development** — building internal training and leadership pipelines to support growth while preserving culture

## Brand Perception

### Customer Views
- Customers in all three focus group locations (Berlin, Killbuck, Wooster) consistently cite **friendliness, personal recognition, and responsiveness** as top strengths
- *"I like the idea when I started with them, I could walk into the branch 2 months later and they knew who I was."* — Wooster customer
- *"Everyone is friendly. You walk in and they say hi and ask personal questions."* — Berlin customer
- Community involvement is visible and appreciated: *"They support everything. They are very 'give back' minded—monetary and involvement."* — Killbuck customer
- Customers in Wooster (the more competitive, less familiar market) show appreciation for the personal touch but this segment is smaller and harder to reach

### Employee Views
- Employees are proud of the 125-year legacy and the bank's community role
- The internal culture is described positively: low drama, collaborative, family-first management philosophy
- Key frustrations: inconsistent communication when change is implemented, uneven volunteer burden (*"It's always the same ones. You get burned out"*), outdated technology, and a frustrating phone system
- Training and development are identified as gaps, particularly for change management
- Employees are enthusiastic about the bank's direction but want clearer vision and more structured support

### Noncustomer Views
- Even noncustomers recognize the bank's community involvement positively: *"They are very active in the community. Any event we do in Sugar Creek, they are always involved."*
- However, noncustomers in Wooster and the broader market perceive the bank as **small, outdated, and too rural** for more sophisticated financial needs
- Awareness drops sharply outside the Holmes County core — the brand equity that exists locally is largely invisible in newer markets

### Director/Board Views
- Directors are deeply aligned with the locally owned, community-first mission
- They view the bank's local decision-making capacity and community commitment as non-negotiable strategic assets
- *"We intend to remain a strong, locally owned bank. Our decisions are based on that."*
- Directors support growth as necessary to sustain local ownership

## Key Takeaways for Financial Analysis

### Deposit & Revenue Drivers
- Core deposit base is heavily relationship-driven — long-tenured, multigenerational customer households in Holmes County represent the foundation of the funding base
- The Amish customer segment is a **distinctive, sticky deposit source** — low digital churn risk, deeply relationship-dependent, unlikely to leave for online competitors
- Community involvement and local reputation are the primary organic customer acquisition channels; no evidence of aggressive rate-based deposit competition strategy
- Small business banking is an active product area; lending flexibility to non-conventional borrowers suggests a meaningful C&I and small business loan portfolio component

### Branch Plans
- The bank is in active expansion mode, with Wooster representing a newer, growth-oriented market
- Focus groups were conducted in Berlin, Killbuck, and Wooster — suggesting at least three branch markets currently active
- Brand investment (this FMS engagement) is intended to support market expansion in northeastern Ohio
- Future branch growth in new markets will require stronger brand awareness infrastructure, as current name recognition is insufficient outside the core

### Financial Goals (as expressed in strategic context)
- **Remain locally owned** — growth is framed explicitly as a prerequisite for sustaining independence
- **Expand market share in northeastern Ohio**, particularly in the Wooster/Wayne County market
- **Improve operational consistency** across branches to protect the service quality that drives retention
- **Modernize technology** (phone systems, digital banking tools) to serve non-Amish and younger customer segments more competitively
- **Strengthen HR and training infrastructure** to reduce turnover in customer-facing roles and protect the personal service brand
- The rebrand is positioned as a **growth enablement initiative**, not merely a cosmetic refresh — FMS frames it as building the capacity to expand while preserving the core identity that has driven 125 years of customer loyalty
