## Background & History

Farmers Bank is an Ohio-based community bank with over a century of operating history. The institution was founded by an alfalfa farmer, and its agricultural heritage remains a defining part of its identity and brand narrative. The bank has grown significantly from its rural roots and now serves multiple markets across Ohio, though this growth has introduced organizational and cultural strain that is a central driver of the current rebranding initiative.

The bank describes itself as deeply embedded in the communities it serves — not merely placing branches where deposits exist, but where relationships and community need exist. Leadership explicitly contrasts this philosophy against typical banking behavior: *"Most banks put branches where there's money. This bank has branches where there are people they care about."*

---

## Geographic Footprint & Market Characteristics

Farmers Bank operates across multiple Ohio markets, with focus group data gathered from three distinct locations:

- **Hurricane, WV/OH area** — appears to be a growth or commercially active market; customer focus group reflected strong business banking relationships and active community presence
- **Pomeroy, OH** — smaller, rural market with strong traditional community values and loyalty-driven customer base; customers cited comfort and familiarity over rate-seeking behavior ("We could have gone elsewhere and gotten a higher rate on a CD, but we feel comfortable here")
- **Nelsonville, OH** — additional market with customer and noncustomer focus groups conducted

All three markets share characteristics of small-town, rural Appalachian Ohio with traditional values, family-oriented demographics, and limited economic diversification. The bank's leadership noted they "do very well in markets that have a traditional flavor" and that they identify with the shared values of these communities. One director noted that the bank's primary market economic development director credited Farmers Bank with facilitating **over 70% of jobs created** in the primary market.

The bank appears to border more economically active markets (reference to Columbus as a competing opportunity center), creating both talent recruitment challenges and potential expansion opportunities.

## Organizational Structure & Leadership

- The organization is described as **relatively flat**: Chairman of the Board and President at the top, with staff below — limited middle management layers
- Leadership maintains an **open-door culture**, though communication downward has degraded in recent years
- A named loan officer, **Jerod**, is specifically called out by a noncustomer in Hurricane for his ability to structure non-traditional loans — indicating strong individual relationship capital among lenders
- Leadership sends **weekly emails** to staff, which are generally well-received but noted as needing a more motivational, vision-oriented tone
- The bank is employee-centric by board and leadership design — benefit decisions are made with employee best interests explicitly considered

Key internal departments represented in discovery process: Executive leadership, Business Banking, Retail Banking, Mortgage, Marketing, HR, and IT.

## Strategic Context

### Why the Rebrand
Farmers Bank is undergoing a brand refresh due to internal and external drift from its core identity. Key drivers include:

- **Structural growing pains** from expansion have created the unintended perception that the bank is *stuck in the past*, even though changes were made to improve customer service
- **Internal culture erosion**: morale challenges, inconsistent branch experiences, communication breakdowns between departments and management layers, and lack of clearly communicated organizational vision
- **Employee disengagement**: staff — particularly longer-tenured employees — express a desire to return to the more familial culture of the bank's earlier years
- **Brand inconsistency**: no clearly articulated brand identity across markets; employees and customers experience the bank differently depending on location

### Growth Strategy
- Expand brand awareness and relevance to **attract new customer segments**, particularly younger demographics and new residents
- Position as the **go-to bank for local businesses**, especially small businesses and nonprofits that need flexible, relationship-driven lending
- Leverage community investment as a **brand equity driver**, not merely a charitable function
- Grow market share in existing footprint before or alongside any geographic expansion
- Become the bank people **hear about when they move to the community**

### Target Segments
- Local small business owners (especially those turned away by larger banks)
- Nonprofits and community organizations
- Families with multi-generational ties to the community
- New residents seeking a community banking relationship
- Potential employees drawn to mission-driven, community-embedded workplaces

## Competitive Positioning & Differentiation

Farmers Bank competes against both regional banks and large national/corporate banks. Key differentiators identified:

| Differentiator | Detail |
|---|---|
| **Local decision-making** | Loan decisions made in-market, not routed to a corporate office; emphasis on finding ways to say *yes* |
| **Personal relationships** | Staff know customers by name, family, history — explicitly contrasted with transactional banking |
| **Flexibility** | Non-cookie-cutter loan structuring; willingness to work with non-traditional borrowers |
| **Community investment** | Visible presence at local events, school programs, sports, Chamber, Rotary; credited with driving local job creation |
| **Speed of service** | Customers cite same-day problem resolution as a key satisfaction driver |

Named competitors are not explicitly listed in the excerpts provided, but the competitive frame is consistently framed as **community bank vs. large corporate bank**. Noncustomers in Pomeroy appear to use other institutions but recognize Farmers Bank's community role. The Hurricane noncustomer focus group specifically cited Jerod by name as a reason someone might consider switching — indicating lender-level brand equity.

One executive noted: *"It's not that we have better products or services. I think our heart is in those things more than our competitors."* This is a candid acknowledgment that product parity exists and that service culture is the true moat.

## Market Dynamics

### Economic Conditions
- Markets served are **rural, Appalachian Ohio** — modest economic base, with limited organic growth drivers
- Local economies appear heavily dependent on small businesses and community institutions
- The bank is described as a significant **economic multiplier** in its primary market (70% of local job creation attributed to bank involvement)
- Leadership acknowledges that talent is lost to Columbus and larger cities — a recurring theme reflecting the economic pressure on the region

### Threats
- **Talent attrition**: employees and potential employees are aware of better economic opportunities in larger metros; retention is an ongoing challenge
- **Generational transition**: as longer-tenured staff age out, the relational culture that differentiates the bank is at risk
- **Digital banking expectations**: not deeply covered in available excerpts, but IT was a focus group participant, suggesting technology capability is a recognized challenge area
- **Brand obscurity among noncustomers**: many noncustomers have limited awareness of what Farmers Bank offers beyond community presence
- **Rate competition**: customers in Pomeroy specifically noted they stayed despite better CD rates elsewhere — loyalty is real but not unconditional

### Opportunities
- **New resident acquisition**: households moving into service markets represent a clean acquisition opportunity with no incumbent relationship
- **Business banking growth**: young business owners and nonprofits cite Farmers as uniquely willing to engage — an underutilized growth channel
- **Employer branding**: positioning as a mission-driven workplace could help with recruitment in a competitive labor market
- **Community brand amplification**: high community investment activity is largely invisible externally; greater communication could convert goodwill into deposits and loans

## Brand Perception

### Customer Views
- Customers consistently cite **personal relationships, friendliness, and problem-solving speed** as top reasons for loyalty
- Strong emotional attachment — customers describe staying with Farmers despite finding better rates elsewhere
- Business banking customers value the bank's willingness to take on non-standard loans and work closely with borrowers (on-site visits, engagement with leadership teams)
- Customers feel *known* — staff awareness of family, community ties, and individual circumstances is a repeatedly cited differentiator
- Representative quote: *"This is the friendliest bank I have ever been to."* (Hurricane customer)

### Employee Views
- Employees take pride in the bank's community role and customer relationships
- Culture is described as family-oriented and employee-centric — leadership is seen as having staff's best interests at heart
- However, significant frustration around **internal communication** — policies, changes, and vision are not consistently communicated downward
- Some employees lack clarity on personal performance goals, reviews, or metrics
- Longer-tenured employees feel the familial culture has eroded over recent years
- Employees express a desire to be more motivated, more included, and more informed
- Representative quote: *"We're proud to be employed by Farmers Bank. We love it here... We want the bank to succeed, but we need help getting there."*

### Noncustomer Views
- Noncustomers broadly recognize Farmers Bank as **active in the community** — visible at events, sponsoring youth programs, supporting local organizations
- Some noncustomers are aware of specific bankers (e.g., Jerod in Hurricane) by reputation for flexible lending
- Limited awareness of specific products or rates — community presence is the primary brand signal for noncustomers
- In Pomeroy, noncustomers describe the bank as *"the driving force in the community"*
- Barrier to switching is not negative perception but rather inertia and existing relationships at competitor institutions

## Key Takeaways for Financial Analysis

### Deposit Drivers
- **Relationship loyalty over rate sensitivity** is the primary retention mechanism — customers in Pomeroy explicitly chose lower CD rates to stay with Farmers Bank
- This dynamic suggests **core deposit stickiness** above peer averages in primary markets, but also potential vulnerability if relationship quality degrades during growth
- Community embeddedness functions as a **low-cost deposit acquisition channel** — event presence, school programs, and business relationships generate organic inflows

### Branch Strategy Implications
- Branch placement philosophy is **relationship- and community-driven, not deposit-density-driven** — this likely results in branches in lower-income or lower-density markets that a purely financial model might not justify
- The three focus group markets (Hurricane, Pomeroy, Nelsonville) represent distinct community profiles; any branch expansion or consolidation decisions should be evaluated against community relationship impact, not just deposit volume
- Internal inconsistency across branches is a noted challenge — standardizing the brand experience is a prerequisite for scaling

### Lending & Business Banking
- Farmers Bank has a **differentiated position in small business and non-traditional lending** — local decision authority, flexibility, and willingness to structure unusual deals are genuine competitive advantages
- The bank is credited with 70%+ of primary market job creation — suggesting outsized CRA activity and community development lending
- Mortgage is an active business line (dedicated focus group) — important in rural Ohio housing markets

### Financial Goals (Inferred)
- Grow market share within existing footprint through brand clarification and culture strengthening
- Improve employee retention to protect relational capital embedded in key staff
- Increase awareness among noncustomers to convert community goodwill into new accounts
- Position the bank for **long-term organic growth** rather than acquisition-driven expansion
- A stronger brand is explicitly linked to the ability to attract both customers and employees in competitive labor and deposit markets

### Risks to Monitor
- Cultural erosion during growth — the bank's primary competitive moat (people and relationships) is at risk if communication and engagement issues are not resolved
- Succession and talent pipeline — individual lenders like Jerod carry significant relationship equity; departure of key staff represents concentrated relationship risk
- Rate environment — loyalty is strong but not absolute; a sustained high-rate environment from competitors could test deposit retention in rate-sensitive segments
